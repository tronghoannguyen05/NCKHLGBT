## Abstract

**Introduction** Sexual and gender minority adults report more anxiety and depression than other adults, but such disparities cannot show whether distress originates in identity or in prejudice. Minority stress theory predicts that minority group members who experience more stigma report more symptoms. We examined which forms of workplace stigma LGBT workers in Vietnam experience and how these forms relate to symptoms.

**Methods** In a cross-sectional survey, 235 LGBT workers reported how often they had experienced eight stigmatizing situations at work during the previous 12 months and completed the PHQ-4. Models adjusted for eight groups of pre-exposure characteristics.

**Results** More than half of participants (57.0%) reported at least one situation. Interpersonal stigma was the most common form (47.2%), particularly unwanted personal questions (33.9%) and offensive jokes (26.8%), whereas career-related (17.4%) and gender expression stigma (18.0%) were less common. A one-unit difference in the stigma index was associated with PHQ-4 scores 1.85 points higher (95% CI [0.71, 2.99]), and the estimate was positive in all 32 specifications. The association was clear for interpersonal stigma and inconclusive for the other forms.

**Conclusion** Symptoms varied with exposure to stigma within the group, a gradient predicted by minority stress theory but not by an identity-based account, although the cross-sectional design does not establish its direction.

**Policy Implications** Elevated symptoms among LGBT workers should not be read as evidence of pathology. Naming sexual orientation and gender identity in the Labor Code, and workplace rules against derogatory jokes and intrusive questions, would address the forms of stigma that LGBT workers most often report.

**Keywords** Minority stress · Workplace stigma · Enacted stigma · LGBT workers · Anxiety · Depression · Vietnam

## Introduction

Sexual minority adults report higher rates of depression and anxiety disorders than heterosexual adults (King et al., 2008; Plöderl & Tremblay, 2015), and bisexual people report higher rates than both heterosexual and gay or lesbian people (Ross et al., 2018). A difference in rates, however, does not identify its source. One interpretation treats sexual or gender minority identity as the origin of psychological distress, whereas the other treats distress as a consequence of the way in which societies treat people who hold these identities (I. H. Meyer, 2003).

The first interpretation has lost its scientific standing. Homosexuality was removed from the diagnostic manual of the American Psychiatric Association in 1973 (Drescher, 2015), and the eleventh revision of the International Classification of Diseases moved gender incongruence out of the chapter on mental disorders (World Health Organization, 2019). Pathologizing views nevertheless persist in social life, including in Vietnam, where school lessons have been documented as presenting same-sex attraction as a mental disorder that can be cured (Human Rights Watch, 2020) and where the Ministry of Health instructed medical facilities in 2022 not to regard homosexuality, bisexuality, or being transgender as illnesses (Ministry of Health of Vietnam, 2022). In such a setting, evidence that LGBT people report more distress can easily be absorbed into the very prejudice that may contribute to that distress.

The workplace is a particularly informative setting in which to examine this question. Adults spend a large share of their waking time at work, interact repeatedly with the same colleagues and supervisors, and cannot easily avoid them. Workplaces also translate prejudice into decisions about assignments, evaluation, and pay. In Vietnam, the Labor Code prohibits employment discrimination on a list of grounds that does not include sexual orientation or gender identity (National Assembly of Vietnam, 2019), so the protection available to LGBT workers depends largely on their employers. However, little is known about which forms of stigma LGBT workers in Vietnam encounter at work or about how these experiences relate to their mental health. Research is therefore needed to describe these experiences and to examine their association with symptoms of anxiety and depression.

### Workplace Stigma and Minority Stress

Link and Phelan (2001) define stigma as the co-occurrence of labeling, stereotyping, separation, status loss, and discrimination in a situation in which power is exercised, so discrimination is one component of a broader process. The current study uses the concept of enacted stigma, which we define as workplace behaviors and conditions that target, or are based on, a worker's sexual orientation, gender identity, or gender expression and that demean, intrude upon, or disadvantage the worker. The minority stress model (I. H. Meyer, 1995, 2003) proposes that such experiences contribute to poorer mental health among sexual minority people because they live in a society that stigmatizes their identities. The model distinguishes distal stressors, such as discrimination and harassment, from proximal stressors, such as expectations of rejection, concealment, and internalized stigma, and it assigns coping and social support a role in altering the link between stress and health. Hatzenbuehler (2009) added that stigma-related stress may impair emotion regulation, interpersonal functioning, and cognition, which in turn may lead to psychopathology. Later work extended the model to structural stigma (Hatzenbuehler, 2016; Hatzenbuehler et al., 2010; Pachankis & Bränström, 2018) and to transgender and gender nonconforming people (Hendricks & Testa, 2012), and it emphasized that minority stress processes should be examined across different social and policy contexts (Frost & Meyer, 2023).

The model yields three types of prediction. At the population level, minority groups should report more symptoms than majority groups. Within a minority group, members exposed to more distal stressors should report more symptoms than other members. Finally, proximal stressors should lie on the path between distal stressors and symptoms. A test of the first prediction requires an equivalently recruited comparison group, and a test of the third requires temporally ordered data, because cross-sectional estimates of mediation can be substantially biased (Maxwell & Cole, 2007). A test of the second prediction requires only variation in exposure within the group. This prediction is also the one that distinguishes the two interpretations described above, because both interpretations predict a disparity between groups, but only the prejudice interpretation predicts that symptoms will vary with exposure to stigma among people who share an LGBT identity. An identity-based account, which locates distress in a characteristic that all members of the group share, offers no reason to expect such a gradient. A gradient would not, however, rule out rival explanations, such as the possibility that people with more symptoms recall more negative events or that a third factor shapes both.

Evidence for a gradient within minority groups is stronger for concurrent than for prospective associations. Across stigmatized groups, people who report more discrimination also report poorer well-being (Lewis et al., 2015), with an average correlation of *r* = −.23 in the meta-analysis of Schmitt et al. (2014). In the same meta-analysis, the association remained negative in longitudinal studies that controlled for earlier well-being (*r* = −.15). Prospective studies of sexual and gender minority people have produced mixed results. Experiences of sexual orientation discrimination have predicted increases in symptoms of depression and anxiety six months later (Dyar et al., 2020, as cited in Herry & Dyar, 2025), whereas an eight-year study of sexual minority people assigned male at birth found that distal stressors were associated with concurrent symptoms but did not predict later change (Pachankis et al., 2018, as cited in Herry & Dyar, 2025). A cross-sectional gradient can therefore establish that stigma and symptoms co-occur, but it cannot establish their temporal order.

Research on minority stress in the workplace has been consistent in direction but limited in design and context. Heterosexism and discrimination at work have been associated with greater psychological distress among lesbian, gay, and bisexual workers in the United States (Velez et al., 2013; Waldo, 1999). A recent systematic review identified 32 studies of 8,369 LGBTQ+ workers, of which 30 were cross-sectional; most of these studies compared LGBTQ+ workers with other workers, and internalized stigma, heterosexism, job stress, and low income were associated with poorer mental health in some groups (Tomic et al., 2025). Studies of variation within LGBTQ+ workforces are fewer; a Canadian survey of 531 LGBTQ workers, for example, examined how precarious employment, industry, and perceived workplace support for LGBTQ people relate to self-reported mental health (Owens et al., 2022). In one of the few studies from Asia, discrimination and other minority stressors among 706 Chinese LGBTQ employees were associated with less favorable perceptions of workplace climate, which were in turn associated with more depressive symptoms, although anxiety was not examined (Lo et al., 2025).

### Forms of Workplace Stigma

Several features of the workplace may change the way in which distal stressors operate. Interaction at work is repeated and difficult to avoid, and leaving a stigmatizing workplace means losing income. Organizations allocate resources hierarchically, so the prejudice of a decision maker can become a formal disadvantage. Organizations can also sanction or tolerate stigma, and gay and lesbian employees have reported less discrimination in organizations with supportive policies and practices (Ragins & Cornwell, 2001). Stigma at work need not take a formal shape, however. In a field study, job applicants presented as gay were not treated differently in formal terms, but they received more negative interpersonal responses (Hebl et al., 2002).

On the basis of this literature, the current study distinguishes three forms of enacted stigma. Interpersonal stigma comprises derogation, such as offensive jokes and comments, which partly overlaps with the microaggressions reviewed by Nadal et al. (2016), and intrusion into privacy, including unwanted personal questions and disclosure of a worker's identity without consent. Career-related stigma comprises formal discrimination in training, evaluation, pay, and work assignments. Gender expression stigma comprises pressure to conform to gender stereotypes and barriers to the expression of gender identity, such as difficulty in using one's name, pronouns, dress, or restroom. Of these three forms, only career-related stigma corresponds to discrimination as it is defined in Vietnam's Labor Code (National Assembly of Vietnam, 2019).

The three forms differ in ways that matter for both measurement and policy. Interpersonal events are recognizable when they occur, because an offensive joke carries its stigmatizing content within itself. A decision about training or pay, by contrast, counts as stigma only if the worker attributes it to their sexual orientation or gender identity, and self-reports of this kind may be more open to the influence of mood and expectations (Lewis et al., 2015). The forms may also differ in their association with distress. In the experimental studies reviewed by Schmitt et al. (2014), manipulating general perceptions of discrimination lowered well-being, whereas attributing a single negative event to discrimination did not, which suggests that frequent everyday events may matter more than rare decisions. A summed score cannot show which forms workers encounter most often or which forms carry an association with symptoms, yet laws and workplace rules address specific behaviors rather than a total score.

### Workplace Stigma in the Vietnamese Context

Vietnam's legal treatment of LGBT people combines elements that point in different directions. The health sector has rejected the pathologizing view (Ministry of Health of Vietnam, 2022), and the Civil Code recognizes gender transition while leaving its regulation to a separate law (National Assembly of Vietnam, 2015, Art. 37). At the same time, the Law on Marriage and Family does not recognize marriage between people of the same sex (National Assembly of Vietnam, 2014, Art. 8(2)), and Article 3(8) of the Labor Code defines employment discrimination through a list of grounds that omits sexual orientation and gender identity (National Assembly of Vietnam, 2019). As a result, LGBT workers have weak grounds for complaint, employers have no specific legal duty to respond, and offensive jokes or intrusive questions are likely to be treated as private matters between colleagues. Because labor law applies uniformly across the country, protection varies mainly across organizations, whereas research in the United States has been able to draw on variation in laws across jurisdictions (Herry & Dyar, 2025; Ragins & Cornwell, 2001).

Reports by international and civil society organizations document the prevalence of stigma in Vietnam. In a national survey of 2,363 LGBT people, about one in three reported having experienced discrimination because of their sexual orientation or gender identity during the previous 12 months, most often in the family, at school, and at work (Luong & Pham, 2015; see also United Nations Development Programme & United States Agency for International Development, 2014). These reports, however, do not distinguish forms of stigma within a domain, nor do they estimate associations with mental health. Peer-reviewed quantitative studies in Vietnam have developed measures for sexual minority women (T. Q. Nguyen, Bandeen-Roche, et al., 2016; T. Q. Nguyen, Poteat, et al., 2016) and have examined mainly proximal processes within single groups, such as internalized homophobia, disclosure, and depressive symptoms among sexual minority women (Tran et al., 2020) and gender dysphoria and psychological distress among transgender people (L. V. Nguyen et al., 2026). Among men who have sex with men in Hanoi, perceived and internalized stigma were associated with sexual risk behavior indirectly through depression and substance use (Ha et al., 2015). To our knowledge, distal stressors within a specific domain of life, such as the workplace, have not yet been measured systematically in Vietnam.

### Current Study

Three gaps in the literature motivate the current study. First, evidence on the mental health of LGBT workers has been reported mainly as differences from other workers (Tomic et al., 2025), and such comparisons cannot distinguish between the pathology and prejudice interpretations. Second, the foundational studies of minority stress at work were conducted in the United States, and evidence from settings in which labor law does not name sexual orientation or gender identity, and in which pathologizing views persist, remains scarce. To our knowledge, no peer-reviewed study in Vietnam has described the forms of enacted stigma that LGBT workers encounter at work or estimated their association with symptoms of anxiety and depression. Third, when workplace stigma is summed into a single score or combined with stigma in other domains (e.g., Herry & Dyar, 2025), it is not possible to identify which forms of behavior workers encounter or which forms carry the association with symptoms, and several studies have measured depressive symptoms only (Lo et al., 2025; Tran et al., 2020).

The present study addresses these gaps by examining two questions among LGBT workers in Vietnam. The first question concerns which forms of enacted stigma LGBT workers experience at work and how often they experience them. The second question concerns how these forms are associated with the severity of anxiety and depressive symptoms after adjustment for characteristics that precede exposure. We use LGBT as an umbrella term for sexual minority and gender minority people, while recognizing that sex assigned at birth, gender identity, and sexual orientation are distinct (National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine, 2022). We examine symptom severity rather than diagnosed disorder, so a higher score indicates neither a disorder nor that an identity is pathological. All analyses compare LGBT workers with one another.

### Hypotheses

The first question is descriptive and was examined without hypothesis tests. For the second question, we hypothesized that, among LGBT workers, more frequent enacted stigma at work would be associated with more severe symptoms of anxiety and depression, as measured by total PHQ-4 scores (H1). As far as we are aware, no prior research has compared the associations of distinct forms of workplace stigma with mental health among LGBT workers in Vietnam. We therefore examined, as an exploratory research question, how interpersonal, career-related, and gender expression stigma are each associated with PHQ-4 scores and whether these associations differ (RQ1). Supplementary exploratory analyses examined each stigmatizing situation separately and three levels of exposure. These exploratory analyses are intended to describe the data and to inform future research, and they were not used to replace or strengthen the test of H1.

## Methods

### Participants and Procedures

The current study used data from a cross-sectional survey of workers in Vietnam. The survey was open to workers of any sexual orientation and gender identity, and the present analyses focus on participants who identified as LGBT. To be eligible, participants had to be 18 years of age or older and have a main income-generating job in Vietnam, as verified by two screening questions. Because no sampling frame exists for LGBT people in Vietnam, participants were recruited through convenience and snowball sampling within community networks. Before answering, participants read an information page that described the purpose of the study, warned that the questionnaire contained sensitive questions, and explained that they could skip any question or stop at any time. Participants indicated consent by continuing to the questionnaire, which did not collect names, contact details, places of residence, or names of employers. ### Measures

#### Demographic and Job Characteristics

**Sex assigned at birth and gender identity.** Participants were asked "What sex were you assigned at birth?" and could answer male, female, or prefer not to answer. They were then asked "What is your current gender identity?" and could answer man, woman, transgender man, transgender woman, nonbinary, another identity, or prefer not to answer. Because few participants reported a transgender, nonbinary, or other gender identity, gender minority status was coded as a binary variable (1 = transgender, nonbinary, or other gender identity; 0 = man or woman). Gender minority status was included as a covariate in all models, but its coefficient is not reported.

**Sexual orientation and LGBT identification.** Participants were asked "What is your sexual orientation?" The response options included heterosexual, gay, lesbian, bisexual, pansexual, another orientation, questioning, and prefer not to answer. Participants were also asked "Do you consider yourself part of the LGBT/LGBTQ+ community?" and could answer yes, no, unsure or questioning, or prefer not to answer. Participants who answered yes formed the LGBT sample. For the analyses, sexual orientation was coded into four groups: gay, lesbian, bisexual or pansexual, and a fourth group that combined other and questioning orientations with the small number of LGBT participants who reported a heterosexual orientation. The categories were dummy coded, with gay participants as the reference group.

**Age, education, relationship status, region of work, and industry.** Participants reported their age group (18–24, 25–34, 35–44, 45–54, or 55 years or older, with the two oldest groups combined), their highest level of education (upper secondary or below, college, university, or postgraduate), and their current relationship status (no partner; a partner, married, or cohabiting; or separated, divorced, or widowed). They also reported the province or city in which they performed most of their work, which was coded as Hanoi, Ho Chi Minh City, Da Nang, or elsewhere, and the industry of their main job, which was coded as finance and banking, information technology, education, health care, trade and services, manufacturing, media and marketing, tourism and hospitality, or another industry.

**Job characteristics.** Participants also reported their total work experience, their position, their employment relationship, whether they were covered by compulsory social insurance, the type and size of their employing organization, and their average weekly working hours in their main job.

**Coding of covariates.** All covariates were categorical, and each item included a "prefer not to answer" option. Under the main rule, "prefer not to answer" was retained as a separate category, and under the alternative rule it was treated as missing; blank responses were treated as missing under both rules. Before estimation, categories with fewer than five participants in the estimation sample were merged according to a rule that uses counts only (Supplementary Methods), because categories with one or two participants produce leverage values close to one, at which heteroskedasticity-consistent standard errors become unstable. Descriptive tables use the original categories.

#### Workplace Stigma

Workplace stigma was assessed only among participants who identified as LGBT. Participants were asked "In the past 12 months at your current workplace, how often have you experienced the following situations?" They were then presented with eight situations: (1) being subjected to jokes, comments, name-calling, or offensive content related to one's sexual orientation, gender identity, or gender expression; (2) being asked unwanted personal questions about one's sexual orientation, gender identity, or romantic life; (3) having one's sexual orientation or gender identity disclosed, or being threatened with disclosure, without consent; (4) being excluded from training, networking, or career development opportunities; (5) receiving an unfair performance evaluation, pay, or promotion; (6) receiving unfavorable shifts, assignments, or work arrangements; (7) being pressured to dress, speak, or behave according to gender stereotypes that did not fit oneself; and (8) having difficulty in using a name, pronouns, dress, restrooms, or facilities consistent with one's gender identity. Situations 4 to 6 asked about treatment that participants believed was related to their sexual orientation, gender identity, or gender expression. For each situation, participants responded on a scale from 0 (*never*) to 4 (*very often*), with intermediate options of 1 (*once*), 2 (*sometimes*), and 3 (*often*). They could also select "not applicable or prefer not to answer," which was coded as missing rather than as zero. The situations were adapted from Waldo (1999) and from behavioral indicators of workplace stigma, were developed together with the project supervisor, were revised after expert consultation, and were piloted with 30 respondents, although the instrument has not been formally validated.

Because each situation represents a distinct type of event, and experiencing one situation does not imply experiencing another, the situations were treated as a formative index rather than as a reflective scale, for which internal consistency is not a criterion of quality (Bollen & Lennox, 1991). For reference, Cronbach's α was .69 among the 112 participants who answered all eight situations. The stigma index (*S*) was computed as the mean frequency across the eight situations when at least six situations were answered, and higher scores indicated more frequent experiences of stigma. In the main sample, 203 participants answered at least seven situations and 112 answered all eight. Situation 8 was the item most often left without a frequency (57 of the 272 participants in the analytic sample), presumably because its content applies mainly to transgender and nonbinary people.

To examine forms of stigma, the situations were grouped into interpersonal stigma (situations 1 to 3), career-related stigma (situations 4 to 6), and gender expression stigma (situations 7 and 8). Each sub-index was computed as the mean of its situations when at least two situations (interpersonal and career-related stigma) or at least one situation (gender expression stigma) were answered. The α values of the three sub-indices were .51, .44, and .42, which are not unusual for formative indices of distinct events. For descriptive purposes, a situation or form was considered experienced when it had occurred at least once (for a form, when its sub-index was above zero), and it was considered experienced often when at least one of its situations was rated 3 or 4.

#### Symptoms of Anxiety and Depression

Symptoms of anxiety and depression were assessed with the Patient Health Questionnaire-4 (PHQ-4; Kroenke et al., 2009), which combines the two anxiety items of the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (Spitzer et al., 2006) with the two depression items of the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (Kroenke et al., 2001). Participants were asked "Over the last 2 weeks, how often have you been bothered by the following problems?" The four items were feeling nervous, anxious, or on edge; not being able to stop or control worrying; feeling down, depressed, or hopeless; and having little interest or pleasure in doing things. Responses ranged from 0 (*not at all*) to 3 (*nearly every day*), with intermediate options of 1 (*several days*) and 2 (*more than half the days*). Item scores were summed to create a total score ranging from 0 to 12, which was computed only when all four items were answered (α = .82 in the main sample), and higher scores indicated more severe symptoms. We found no validation study of the Vietnamese PHQ-4, and the closest evidence is the evaluation of the PHQ-9 among Vietnamese sexual minority women (T. Q. Nguyen, Bandeen-Roche, et al., 2016), from which the two depression items are drawn. Scores were therefore treated as a continuous measure of symptom severity. For description only, scores were also classified into the conventional categories of normal (0–2), mild (3–5), moderate (6–8), and severe (9–12) symptom levels (Kroenke et al., 2009).

#### Other Measures and Response Quality

The questionnaire also contained short scales on identity concealment and on perceived enforcement of diversity, equity, and inclusion policies at work. These constructs are outside the scope of this article, and the two scales were used only to assess response quality. Participants were flagged if they chose the same option for every item of all three Likert scales in the questionnaire, namely the PHQ-4 and the two scales named above, or if they gave clearly contradictory answers, such as reporting the youngest age group together with a senior management position. Flagged responses were retained in the main analyses and excluded in a sensitivity analysis.

### Analytic Plan

All analyses were conducted in Stata 17 with the user-written commands sensemakr and boottest, and an independent replication in Python reproduced every estimate that does not depend on random draws. The main analyses used complete cases, and the potential influence of missing data was assessed by comparing participants with and without complete data and by re-estimating the main model after multiple imputation. Prior to the primary analyses, descriptive statistics were computed for symptoms and for each situation and form of stigma, with the participants who answered each item as the denominator. Mean PHQ-4 scores and the percentage of participants scoring 6 or more were also compared between participants who did and did not experience each form, without adjustment and without hypothesis tests. Next, a regression of PHQ-4 scores on the pre-exposure characteristics was estimated to identify demographic differences in symptoms (Table S1).

H1 was tested with the model PHQᵢ = α + η·Sᵢ + γ′Xᵢᴰ + εᵢ, estimated by ordinary least squares, in which η is the expected difference in PHQ-4 scores between two LGBT workers whose stigma indices differ by one unit on the 0–4 scale and who share the same pre-exposure characteristics (Xᴰ). Because the design is cross-sectional and both measures are self-reported, η is interpreted as an adjusted association rather than as a causal effect. Standard errors were heteroskedasticity-consistent of type HC3, which Long and Ervin (2000) recommend for samples of fewer than 250 observations, and this choice was made before estimation regardless of the results of heteroskedasticity tests. H1 was considered supported if η was positive and statistically significant in a two-sided test at the 5% level.

Covariates were selected with a causal diagram (Figure 1; Greenland et al., 1999), following the principle of adjusting for pre-exposure causes of the exposure, the outcome, or both (VanderWeele, 2019). The pre-exposure characteristics comprised eight groups: age group, education, sex assigned at birth, gender minority status, sexual orientation group, relationship status, region of work, and industry. Gender minority status and sexual orientation group precede workplace experiences and may shape both exposure to stigma and symptoms (Ross et al., 2018). Industry was treated as a pre-exposure characteristic because most workers enter a sector before their experiences at a particular workplace, although some workers may move to sectors that they perceive as more accepting, and a sensitivity analysis therefore omitted it. Two further covariates are open to similar doubts, because relationship status may partly reflect symptoms and region of work may reflect moves to cities that are perceived as more accepting. Other job characteristics (Xᴶ) may confound the association, because the type and size of an organization may shape exposure. However, they may also lie on the path between stigma and symptoms, because stigma may push workers into lower positions or less secure employment, and adjusting for a variable on this path would remove part of the association of interest (Angrist & Pischke, 2009). Job characteristics were therefore added only in a second specification, which indicates how far the association may run through these characteristics.

To distinguish the absence of an association from an imprecise estimate, the smallest effect size of interest was set at 1.0 PHQ-4 point per unit of *S*. This value corresponds to about 0.14 standard deviations of the PHQ-4 per standard deviation of *S* in the main sample and is smaller than the average association between perceived discrimination and well-being reported by Schmitt et al. (2014). Alongside the conventional test, H1 was examined with the two one-sided tests procedure (Lakens, 2017; Lakens et al., 2018). The absence of a practically meaningful association would be concluded only if the 90% confidence interval lay entirely within ±1.0, a positive association would be concluded if the 95% confidence interval excluded zero and the estimate was positive, and the result would be considered inconclusive if the interval contained both zero and values beyond the bound. The same reading of confidence intervals was applied to the exploratory analyses. After estimation, the minimum detectable effect at 5% significance and 80% power was calculated as 2.8 times the standard error of η.

RQ1 used the 233 participants in the main sample with valid scores on all three sub-indices. Each sub-index was standardized within this sample, so that its coefficient represents the difference in PHQ-4 points per standard deviation and can be compared across forms that occur at different frequencies. One model was estimated for each sub-index with the same covariates as the model for H1, and a joint model entered the three sub-indices together. Because a difference in statistical significance between two coefficients is not evidence that the coefficients differ (Gelman & Stern, 2006), the three pairwise differences were tested directly in the joint model. In supplementary exploratory analyses, one model was estimated for each situation, in which *S* was replaced with an indicator of having experienced that situation at least once. Situations that were experienced by fewer than 10 participants, or not experienced by fewer than 10, were not estimated, so that small cells would not be disclosed. A further model replaced *S* with indicators of low and high exposure, with no exposure as the reference category and the median of *S* among exposed participants as the cut point. H1 was treated as the single hypothesis test and was not adjusted for multiple testing, whereas the *p* values of the three separate RQ1 models and of the models for individual situations were adjusted with the Benjamini and Hochberg (1995) procedure.

Several sensitivity analyses examined whether the association depended on analytic choices. These analyses (1) added job characteristics; (2) treated "prefer not to answer" as missing; (3) separated any exposure from intensity among exposed participants and fitted a restricted cubic spline with knots at the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles of *S* among exposed participants; (4) estimated a fractional logit model for PHQ-4 scores divided by 12 and reported the average marginal effect multiplied by 12 (Papke & Wooldridge, 1996); (5) used multiple imputation by chained equations with 20 imputed datasets among the 300 LGBT participants, imputing PHQ-4 scores, *S*, and missing covariates (White et al., 2011); (6) excluded flagged responses; (7) excluded observations with a Cook's distance above 4/*n*; (8) used a restricted wild bootstrap with Webb weights and 9,999 replications (Davidson & Flachaire, 2008; Roodman et al., 2019); and (9) omitted industry from the covariates. A specification curve (Simonsohn et al., 2020) combined four constructions of the stigma index (the eight-situation mean, the mean of situations 1 to 7 with at least four answered, the number of situations experienced, and the eight-situation mean among participants who answered all eight situations), two sets of covariates, two definitions of the LGBT group (self-identification alone, or self-identification together with a non-heterosexual orientation or a minority gender identity), and the inclusion or exclusion of transgender and nonbinary participants, which yielded 32 specifications. Every result is reported, and no threshold was set for the share of specifications that must agree.

Four sources of bias could inflate η, namely minority stress outside work that was not measured, reverse causation, recall bias, and common method variance (Podsakoff et al., 2003). The stigma and PHQ-4 sections used different response scales and reference periods, but the stigma section immediately preceded the PHQ-4, and recalling stigmatizing events just before rating one's symptoms may raise symptom reports (Schwarz, 1999). Sensitivity to unmeasured confounding was quantified with the omitted variable bias framework of Cinelli and Hazlett (2020), which reports the partial *R*² of *S* with PHQ-4 scores and the robustness values at which an omitted confounder would reduce η to zero or make its 95% confidence interval include zero. These values were compared with the explanatory power of sex assigned at birth and of education, each entered as a group of dummy variables, at one, two, and three times their observed strength. Instrumental variables, propensity score matching, selection models, Harman's single factor test, and coefficient stability bounds (Oster, 2019) were not used, for reasons given in the Supplementary Methods. Diagnostics of the main model, namely the Breusch-Pagan test, generalized variance inflation factors (Fox & Monette, 1992), Ramsey's RESET test, Cook's distance, and leverage, were descriptive and did not change the estimator. Four further sensitivity analyses were specified after the main results were known, and they are reported separately as post hoc analyses (Table S6). Finally, every table by sexual orientation, gender identity, demographic group, or workplace characteristic, and every table of stigma experiences, suppresses cells with fewer than 10 participants together with any cell from which a suppressed count could be derived.

The mean PHQ-4 score in the main sample was 3.43 (*SD* = 3.25, range = 0–12; Table 3). Almost half of the participants (46.0%) scored in the normal range, 31.1% reported mild symptoms, 16.2% reported moderate symptoms, and 6.8% reported severe symptoms, so that 23.0% scored 6 or more. ## Discussion

The current study provides support for H1 and, to our knowledge, offers the first description of the forms of enacted stigma that LGBT workers in Vietnam encounter at work. More than half of the participants had experienced at least one stigmatizing situation during the previous 12 months, and interpersonal stigma, particularly unwanted personal questions and offensive jokes, was the most common form. As hypothesized, participants who reported stigma more often also reported more severe symptoms of anxiety and depression, and the estimate changed little across covariate sets, codings of missing responses, estimators, checks for influential observations, and 32 alternative specifications. In the exploratory analyses, the association was clear for interpersonal stigma and inconclusive for career-related and gender expression stigma. These results add to the literature on minority stress at work (Tomic et al., 2025; Velez et al., 2013; Waldo, 1999) and extend it to a setting in which labor law offers LGBT workers no specific protection.

### Forms of Workplace Stigma among LGBT Workers in Vietnam

Stigma at work was common but mostly infrequent, as most exposed participants reported one or two situations and fewer than three in ten participants reported any situation often. The share of participants who reported at least one situation (57.0%) is higher than the share of respondents in a national survey who reported discrimination in any domain of life during the previous 12 months, which was about one in three (Luong & Pham, 2015). The two figures are not directly comparable, however, because the current measure includes behaviors such as intrusive questions and jokes, which respondents may not label as discrimination, and because the two samples were recruited in different ways.

The distribution across forms is the clearest descriptive finding of the study. Interpersonal stigma was reported more than twice as often as career-related stigma, which is the only form that Vietnam's Labor Code would recognize as discrimination. This pattern resembles evidence that interpersonal bias persists in situations in which formal discrimination is not observed (Hebl et al., 2002). Three features of the setting and of the measurement may contribute to the pattern. First, interpersonal events are recognizable as they happen, whereas a decision about training or pay counts as stigma only if the worker attributes it to their identity, and workers often cannot observe how their colleagues were treated (Lewis et al., 2015). Second, because Vietnamese law does not recognize marriage between people of the same sex (National Assembly of Vietnam, 2014), routine questions about romantic life and marriage plans may repeatedly confront LGBT workers with a choice between concealment and disclosure. Third, workers who conceal their identity may be spared stigma that is directed at it, so the low prevalence of some forms may partly reflect concealment rather than acceptance; in countries with high structural stigma, concealment has been found to protect sexual minority people partially against discrimination and victimization (Pachankis & Bränström, 2018).

Findings on gender expression stigma require particular caution, because situation 8 was left without a frequency by about one in five participants, presumably because it applies mainly to transgender and nonbinary people. Its prevalence among the workers to whom it applies therefore cannot be estimated from the current sample, and because the group of transgender and nonbinary participants was small, the experiences of these workers require studies with targeted recruitment.

### A Gradient in Symptoms within the LGBT Group

The gradient within the group is the prediction that distinguishes the two interpretations described in the Introduction. Both an identity-based interpretation and a prejudice interpretation predict that LGBT people report more symptoms than heterosexual and cisgender people, but only the prejudice interpretation expects symptoms to vary with exposure to stigma among people who share an LGBT identity. The gradient observed in the current study is therefore consistent with minority stress theory (I. H. Meyer, 2003) and with its account of distal stressors, and it is not predicted by an account that locates distress in sexual or gender minority identity itself. The bivariate correlation between the stigma index and PHQ-4 scores (*r* = .21) is of the same magnitude as the average association between perceived discrimination and well-being in the meta-analysis of Schmitt et al. (2014), and its direction matches that found in workplace studies in the United States (Velez et al., 2013; Waldo, 1999). The shape of the association was broadly linear, as high exposure was associated with clearly higher scores and the spline provided no evidence of nonlinearity, but the data could not distinguish whether the association reflects having any experience of stigma or the frequency of such experiences.

The gradient is necessary for the prejudice interpretation, but it is not sufficient to establish that stigma raises symptoms, because three alternative explanations remain. First, the direction of the association may run partly from symptoms to reports, as people with more symptoms may recall or interpret more events as stigmatizing, or may be treated worse by colleagues. Second, a factor that the models did not measure, such as rejection by family or stigma in other domains of life, may shape both workplace experiences and symptoms. Third, because the stigma section immediately preceded the PHQ-4, recalling stigmatizing events may have raised the symptom ratings that followed (Schwarz, 1999). The analyses address these alternatives only in part. The sensitivity analysis shows that a confounder would need to be several times as strongly related to stigma and symptoms as education to remove the association, but it cannot show that no such confounder exists, and family rejection could plausibly be that strong. The data also cannot separate the first and third explanations from an influence of stigma on symptoms.

### Interpersonal Stigma and Mental Health

The association was clear for interpersonal stigma, which comprised offensive jokes or comments, unwanted personal questions about sexual orientation, gender identity, or romantic life, and disclosure without consent. Interpersonal situations were also the most common, so their association with symptoms was estimated most precisely. The confidence intervals for career-related and gender expression stigma include values as large as the coefficient for interpersonal stigma, none of the pairwise differences could be distinguished from zero, and the low internal consistency of the sub-indices adds noise to all three estimates. The results therefore do not show that formal discrimination or gender expression stigma is less strongly associated with symptoms. Rather, they show that the most common form of stigma in this sample is also a form that clearly accompanies symptoms.

This finding is consistent with evidence that microaggressions accompany psychological distress (Nadal et al., 2016), and it fits the Vietnamese context described in the Introduction. Where labor law does not name sexual orientation or gender identity, jokes and intrusive questions are especially likely to be treated as private matters between colleagues, and the worker who is targeted has few formal means of response. Behaviors that may be dismissed as trivial were thus the most frequent form of stigma reported by LGBT workers, and they clearly accompanied symptoms of anxiety and depression.

### Policy Implications

The first implication concerns the interpretation of mental health disparities. Higher symptom levels among LGBT people are sometimes read as evidence that LGBT identities are pathological. In the current sample, however, symptom levels varied with how often workers were stigmatized at work, which an identity-based account would not predict. This finding is consistent with the position of the Ministry of Health of Vietnam (2022) that sexual and gender minority identities should not be regarded as illnesses, and it suggests that health and workplace programs should address the conditions that LGBT workers face rather than treat LGBT identity as a risk factor in itself.

The second implication concerns labor law, because Article 3(8) of the Labor Code defines discrimination in employment through a list of grounds that does not name sexual orientation or gender identity (National Assembly of Vietnam, 2019). Adding these grounds would give workers a basis for complaints about formal decisions such as those concerning training, evaluation, and pay. The forms of stigma that LGBT workers reported most often, however, were interpersonal, and they fall outside the narrow definition of discrimination as distinction, exclusion, or preference. Effective protection would therefore also require rules on workplace conduct that cover derogatory jokes, intrusive questioning, and the disclosure of a worker's sexual orientation or gender identity without consent.

The third implication concerns employers, who can act without waiting for legal change because protection in Vietnam depends largely on the individual organization. Codes of conduct can name derogatory jokes and intrusive questions about sexual orientation, gender identity, and private life as unacceptable, protect information about a worker's identity, and provide a reporting channel that workers regard as safe. The current study does not show whether such measures weaken the association between stigma and symptoms, so evaluations should be built into their introduction. All of these implications assume that the association reflects, at least in part, an influence of stigma on symptoms, which the cross-sectional design cannot establish, and they should be read with that qualification.

### Limitations and Future Directions

Although the current study provides new evidence on the forms of workplace stigma that LGBT workers in Vietnam experience and on their association with mental health, the results should be considered within the context of several limitations. First, the design was cross-sectional, so the direction of the association cannot be established. Longitudinal designs that measure stigma and symptoms repeatedly, including diary designs that capture events close to the time at which they occur, would allow the temporal order to be examined. Second, exposure and outcome were reported by the same person in a single questionnaire, and because the stigma section immediately preceded the PHQ-4, the data cannot test whether this order raised symptom reports. Future surveys should separate the two sections or randomize their order.

Third, participants were recruited through convenience and snowball sampling. Participation may depend on both stigma and distress (Hernán et al., 2004), and the employment criterion excluded workers who had left their jobs, possibly because of stigma or distress. The sample was concentrated in Ho Chi Minh City, among university graduates, and in education and media, so the prevalence estimates may not generalize to LGBT workers in rural areas, in informal employment, or in other sectors. Snowball recruitment may also produce correlated errors among participants from the same network or workplace. Because no network or workplace identifier was recorded, standard errors could not be clustered and may be understated, and the wild bootstrap addresses inference in small samples but not clustering.

Fourth, the measures have limitations, because the stigma situations have not been formally validated and each form was represented by only two or three situations, which resulted in sub-indices with low internal consistency. Exclusion from training was too rare to analyze, and the small number of career-related events limited the precision of the estimates for that form. Situation 8 applies mainly to transgender and nonbinary people, and although coding "not applicable" as missing avoids treating it as an absence of stigma, the index of about one in five participants rests on the remaining situations. In addition, no validation study of the Vietnamese PHQ-4 was available. Future research should validate a Vietnamese measure of workplace stigma that includes more situations for each form and should use longer symptom scales.

Fifth, the covariates did not capture minority stress outside work, such as rejection by family. Relationship status may partly reflect symptoms, and region of work and industry may reflect moves to places or sectors that are perceived as more accepting, although omitting industry, or relationship status and region, changed the estimate little. The models adjusted for eight groups of covariates in a sample of 235 participants, which required sparse categories to be merged, and the sensitivity analysis indicates how strong an omitted confounder would need to be, but it cannot show that none exists. Finally, the group of transgender and nonbinary participants was too small to be analyzed separately, and the barriers related to gender identity that these workers face may differ in kind from the stigma that cisgender LGBT workers encounter. Replication in a new and larger sample, with targeted recruitment of transgender and nonbinary workers, would provide a stronger test of the associations reported here.

### Conclusions

Overall, the current study documents that more than half of the LGBT workers in this Vietnamese sample experienced stigma at work during the previous 12 months, most often in the form of unwanted personal questions and offensive jokes. Workers who encountered stigma more often reported more symptoms of anxiety and depression, and this association remained similar across a wide range of analytic choices. In exploratory analyses, the association was clear for interpersonal stigma, whereas the results for career-related and gender expression stigma were inconclusive. A gradient of this kind within the group is predicted by minority stress theory and not by an account that treats sexual or gender minority identity as the source of distress. Although the cross-sectional design leaves the direction of the association open, the findings direct attention away from LGBT identity and toward the treatment of LGBT workers, including the everyday interpersonal behavior that current Vietnamese labor law does not address.

### Supplementary Methods

**S1. Merging sparse covariate categories.** In each estimation sample, a covariate category with fewer than five participants was merged before estimation. For ordered variables (age group, education, work experience, organization size, and working hours), the category was merged with the adjacent category nearer the median. For nominal variables, it was merged into a pooled "other" category. A sparse "prefer not to answer" category was merged into the pooled category if one existed and otherwise into the largest category. The rule uses counts only. In the main sample, the "prefer not to answer" category of age group was merged into the 25–34 category, and the corresponding category of sexual orientation group was merged into the group of other or questioning orientations. Among the job characteristics, sparse categories of work experience, position, employment relationship, organization type, social insurance coverage, and working hours were merged.

**S2. Approaches not used.** Instrumental variables were not used, because no available variable plausibly affects stigma without also affecting symptoms directly. Propensity score matching rests on the same assumption of selection on observed variables as regression adjustment and is poorly suited to a continuous exposure. A Heckman selection model requires data on nonparticipants, which do not exist for this survey. Harman's single factor test cannot detect common method variance (Podsakoff et al., 2003), and the coefficient stability approach of Oster (2019) rests on an assumption of proportional selection that is difficult to justify for a self-reported psychological outcome.
