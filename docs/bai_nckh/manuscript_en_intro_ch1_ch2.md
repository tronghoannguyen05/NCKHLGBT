## INTRODUCTION

Sexual minority adults report higher rates of depression and anxiety disorders than heterosexual adults, a pattern documented in systematic reviews and meta-analyses (King et al., 2008; Plöderl & Tremblay, 2015). A difference in rates, however, does not identify its source. The same disparity can be read in two opposing ways. One reading treats sexual or gender minority identity as the origin of psychological distress. The other attributes distress to the way societies treat people who hold these identities (I. H. Meyer, 2003).

In Vietnam, the first reading is not merely historical. In 2022, the Ministry of Health issued an official letter instructing medical facilities not to regard homosexuality, bisexuality, or being transgender as illnesses (Ministry of Health of Vietnam, 2022). The need for such an instruction indicates that pathologizing views still shape practice. In that environment, evidence of elevated anxiety or depression among LGBT people risks being absorbed into the very prejudice that may help to produce it.

Minority stress theory turns this interpretive dispute into a testable prediction. If symptoms arise partly from prejudice, then within a minority group, members who encounter stigma more often would be expected to report more symptoms (I. H. Meyer, 2003). A pathologizing account locates the source of symptoms in identity, which all members of the group share; it therefore offers no reason to expect symptoms to vary with exposure to stigma. The test is asymmetric. A precisely estimated absence of such a gradient would remove an empirical pillar of the minority stress account in the setting studied. A gradient would be consistent with the account, but it would not by itself exclude rival explanations, such as the possibility that people with more symptoms recall or report more negative events.

The workplace is a particularly informative setting for this test. Adults spend a large share of their waking hours at work, interact repeatedly with the same colleagues and supervisors, and cannot easily avoid those interactions. Workplaces are also sites where prejudice can be converted into decisions about task assignment, evaluation, training, and pay. In Vietnam, the Labor Code prohibits discrimination in employment on a list of grounds that does not explicitly include sexual orientation or gender identity (National Assembly of Vietnam, 2019). The protection that LGBT workers receive therefore depends heavily on the policies and practices of individual employers.

Existing evidence does not yet answer the question for Vietnam, and it answers it only partially elsewhere. The foundational studies of minority stress at work were conducted mainly in the United States (Ragins & Cornwell, 2001; Velez et al., 2013; Waldo, 1999). A recent systematic review of LGBTQ+ workers' mental health found that most studies were cross-sectional and reported their findings chiefly as disparities between LGBTQ+ and non-LGBTQ+ workers (Tomic et al., 2025). In some studies, discrimination is summed across life domains, with work as one domain among several (e.g., Herry & Dyar, 2025). In Vietnam, reports by international and civil society organizations have documented discrimination at work (Institute for Studies of Society, Economy and Environment [iSEE], 2015; United Nations Development Programme [UNDP] & United States Agency for International Development [USAID], 2014), whereas peer-reviewed quantitative studies have focused on internalized stigma and self-stigma, usually within a single sexual or gender minority group (Ngo et al., 2026; Tran et al., 2020).

This study asks the following question: *Among LGBT workers in Vietnam, is the frequency of enacted stigma at work associated with the severity of anxiety and depressive symptoms, after adjustment for characteristics that precede exposure?* The question deliberately does not ask why LGBT people have poorer mental health. It asks how far symptom levels track exposure to prejudice. The study has three objectives. The first is to estimate the association of workplace stigma with total symptom severity and, separately, with anxiety and depressive symptoms. The second is to identify which forms of stigmatizing behavior accompany higher symptom levels. The third, which is exploratory, is to examine whether the association is weaker where workers perceive diversity, equity, and inclusion (DEI) policies to be enforced, and whether it differs by sex assigned at birth and sexual orientation group. The study also assesses whether the data are consistent with a pathway through identity concealment.

The data come from a cross-sectional survey that yielded 850 responses. The main analytic sample comprises 278 LGBT workers. Analyses are conducted within the LGBT group, comparing workers who differ in their exposure to stigma and adjusting for characteristics that precede exposure; the estimation strategy is set out in Chapter 3. The analysis plan was registered on the Open Science Framework before the main model was estimated [TO BE CONFIRMED: registration submitted; link and date]. Because the mental health question was placed at the center of the analysis after the author had partial contact with the data, the study is presented as exploratory research with a publicly registered analysis plan rather than as a confirmatory test.

The study aims to contribute in five ways. Theoretically, it tests the within-group prediction of minority stress theory in a hierarchical setting in which distal stressors can operate through both interpersonal behavior and formal decisions, and it clarifies that identity concealment can stand on either side of enacted stigma: as a response to it and as a way of avoiding it. Empirically, it provides estimates from LGBT workers of several sexual and gender minority groups in a country whose labor law does not name discrimination based on sexual orientation or gender identity, where concealment may be common, and where pathologizing views remain in public discourse. For measurement, it uses an index of concrete workplace situations that separates interpersonal behavior from personnel decisions that require respondents to attribute a motive, which permits an indirect check on attribution bias; the index has not been validated, and the manuscript treats this as a limitation.

Methodologically, the study is designed to define its estimand in advance, select covariates by temporal order, correct for multiple testing within families of hypotheses, quantify sensitivity to unmeasured confounding, and disclose the history of the analysis. Practically, if the association is confirmed, the findings would suggest that workplace stigma, including behaviors often dismissed as trivial, belongs within the scope of occupational mental health.

The manuscript is organized in five chapters. Chapter 1 establishes the research problem: the mental health disparity, its competing interpretations, and the Vietnamese context. Chapter 2 develops the theoretical basis, synthesizes evidence on the mechanisms linking workplace stigma to symptoms, and identifies the research gaps. Chapter 3 presents the conceptual framework, the hypotheses, and the methods. Chapter 4 reports the results in the order of the hypotheses. Chapter 5 interprets the findings in relation to theory and prior evidence and discusses implications and limitations. A brief Conclusion closes the manuscript.

## CHAPTER 1. MENTAL HEALTH DISPARITIES, COMPETING INTERPRETATIONS, AND THE VIETNAMESE CONTEXT

This chapter establishes the research problem. It first clarifies the concepts that define the study population and the outcome (Section 1.1). It then reviews the evidence on mental health disparities and explains why their interpretation, rather than their existence, is the open question (Section 1.2). Section 1.3 examines the legal, policy, and empirical context in Vietnam, and Section 1.4 states the problem that the rest of the manuscript addresses.

### 1.1. Key Concepts

#### 1.1.1. Sexual and Gender Minorities

Sex, gender identity, and sexual orientation are related but conceptually distinct, and current measurement guidance recommends assessing them separately (National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine, 2022). Sex assigned at birth is the sex recorded at birth, usually on the basis of physical characteristics. Gender identity is a person's internal sense of their own gender, which may or may not correspond to the sex assigned at birth. Gender expression refers to the ways in which a person communicates gender through appearance, voice, or behavior. Sexual orientation encompasses romantic or sexual attraction together with the identities and behaviors associated with it. This manuscript uses SOGIE to refer jointly to sexual orientation, gender identity, and gender expression.

Sexual minorities are people whose sexual orientation is not heterosexual, such as gay men, lesbian women, and bisexual or pansexual people. Gender minorities are people whose gender identity differs from the sex assigned at birth, such as transgender and nonbinary people. The study uses LGBT as an umbrella term for both groups, in line with the self-identification item in the questionnaire ("Do you identify as part of the LGBT/LGBTQ+ community?"). This usage carries two limitations. First, LGBT people are not a homogeneous group. Bisexual, gay or lesbian, and transgender people may differ in the visibility of their identities, in the forms of stigma they encounter, and in their symptom levels, so an estimate for the whole group is an average over different experiences. Second, self-identification does not coincide perfectly with SOGIE attributes: a person with a non-heterosexual orientation may not identify with the LGBT community, and the reverse is also possible. Because the questionnaire records sex assigned at birth, current gender identity, sexual orientation, and LGBT self-identification separately, the sensitivity of the results to the definition of the group can be examined, as described in Chapter 3.

#### 1.1.2. Symptoms Rather Than Disorders

The study measures symptom severity, not psychiatric disorder. Anxiety disorders and depressive disorders are clinical diagnoses that require assessment by a trained professional against diagnostic criteria. Symptoms such as uncontrollable worry, low mood, or loss of interest can occur at varying levels in anyone and can arise from many sources, including the conditions in which people live. The PHQ-4 was developed as an ultra-brief screener consisting of two anxiety items and two depression items (Kroenke et al., 2009). In this study it serves as a continuous measure of symptom severity over the preceding two weeks.

The distinction matters for the question posed in the title. A higher symptom score does not indicate a disorder, and it says nothing about whether an identity is pathological. The empirical question is whether a specific social condition, stigma at work, accompanies higher symptom levels.

### 1.2. Mental Health Disparities and Their Interpretation

That sexual minority people experience more depression and anxiety than heterosexual people is a consistently replicated finding in psychiatric epidemiology. A systematic review and meta-analysis by King et al. (2008) estimated that the risk of depression and anxiety disorders among lesbian, gay, and bisexual people was at least 1.5 times that among heterosexual people. A later systematic review found elevated risks of depression, anxiety, and suicidal behavior among sexual minority men and women, adolescents and adults, across many geographic regions (Plöderl & Tremblay, 2015). The disparity is not uniform. A meta-analysis by Ross et al. (2018) showed that bisexual people have higher rates of depression and anxiety than both heterosexual and gay or lesbian people. This finding is directly relevant here, because bisexual and pansexual workers form the largest group in the present sample.

A disparity is something to be explained, not an explanation. For most of the twentieth century, psychiatry treated homosexuality itself as a disorder. The American Psychiatric Association removed homosexuality from its diagnostic manual in 1973, after weighing theories that pathologized homosexuality against theories that regarded it as a normal variant of human sexuality (Drescher, 2015). In 1990, the World Health Organization adopted the tenth revision of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10), in which homosexuality was no longer a diagnosis. ICD-11, in force since 2022, moved gender incongruence out of the chapter on mental, behavioural, and neurodevelopmental disorders and into a chapter on conditions related to sexual health (World Health Organization, 2019). These decisions reflect scientific consensus. They do not, on their own, remove pathologizing interpretations from social life.

The question "pathology or prejudice?" should therefore be understood as a question about how data are interpreted, not as a contest between two scientific theories of equal standing. Sexual orientation and gender identity are not disorders, and this study does not treat identity as a pathology to be explained. The meaningful empirical question is whether symptoms vary with the social conditions that LGBT people experience. Framing the problem in this way imposes two requirements on the design.

The first requirement is that the analysis be conducted within the LGBT group. Only when group membership is held constant can variation in symptoms be compared with variation in exposure to stigma. A comparison between LGBT and heterosexual workers would only restate the size of the disparity, which existing reviews have already established. It would also fail to discriminate between the two readings, because both predict a disparity.

The second requirement is to identify the rival explanation correctly. For a within-group gradient between stigma and symptoms, the most serious alternative is not that "identity causes symptoms." The most serious alternatives take two forms. One is that symptoms shape how stigma is experienced and reported: people with depressive symptoms may recall more negative events, anxious people may read ambiguous behavior as hostile, and symptoms may also make a person a more likely target of mistreatment. The other is that a third factor, such as stress within the family, is related both to stigma at work and to symptoms. These are problems of identification, and Chapter 3 treats them as such.

### 1.3. The Vietnamese Context

#### 1.3.1. Legal and Policy Configuration

Vietnam's legal treatment of LGBT people combines elements that pull in different directions. The health sector has formally abandoned the pathologizing view: Official Letter No. 4132/BYT-PC of 3 August 2022 instructs medical facilities not to regard homosexuality, bisexuality, or being transgender as illnesses (Ministry of Health of Vietnam, 2022). The Civil Code recognizes gender transition and the right of people who have transitioned to register a change of civil status, while leaving the regulation of transition to a separate law (National Assembly of Vietnam, 2015, Art. 37). Labor law, by contrast, does not name sexual orientation or gender identity. Article 3(8) of the 2019 Labor Code defines discrimination in employment as distinction, exclusion, or preference on grounds that include race, color, national or social origin, ethnicity, sex, age, maternity status, marital status, religion, belief, political opinion, disability, family responsibilities, HIV status, and trade union activity, where the effect is to impair equality of opportunity in employment or occupation (National Assembly of Vietnam, 2019). The list includes sex but does not explicitly include sexual orientation or gender identity. The 2014 Law on Marriage and Family states that the state does not recognize marriage between people of the same sex (National Assembly of Vietnam, 2014, Art. 8(2)).

This configuration shapes the research problem in two ways. The first concerns the capacity to name experience. When labor law does not name discrimination based on sexual orientation or gender identity, workers have weak grounds for complaint, and employers have no specific legal duty to respond. Offensive jokes or intrusive questions are especially likely to be treated as private matters between individuals.

The second concerns variation across workplaces. In the absence of a general legal requirement, DEI policies are voluntary choices made by individual organizations. This applies even to benefits tied to marital status: because same-sex marriage is not recognized, whether a worker's same-sex partner receives family-related benefits depends on the employer. Similarly, because the regulation of gender transition has been left to a separate law, whether a transgender worker can use a name, dress, and facilities consistent with their gender identity depends largely on the practices of the workplace. The protection that LGBT workers receive may therefore vary considerably between workplaces. This variation is what makes it possible to study perceived DEI enforcement as a workplace-level condition.

The legal configuration also distinguishes Vietnam from the settings that have produced most of the evidence on minority stress at work. In the United States, gay and lesbian employees reported more discrimination when their workplaces were not covered by protective legislation (Ragins & Cornwell, 2001), and sexual minority women and gender diverse individuals assigned female at birth who lived in states with more nondiscrimination policies reported less sexual orientation discrimination (Herry & Dyar, 2025). These studies exploit policy variation across jurisdictions within one country. In Vietnam, labor law applies uniformly across the country, so variation in protection lies mainly at the level of the organization.

#### 1.3.2. Evidence on Stigma and Mental Health in Vietnam

Evidence on LGBT people in Vietnam comes from two bodies of work that serve different purposes. Reports by international and civil society organizations describe the prevalence and forms of stigma. The UNDP and USAID (2014) country report reviewed the situation of LGBT people in employment, education, health care, family life, and the media. A national survey of 2,363 respondents across 63 provinces found that about one in three felt they had experienced discrimination because of their sexual orientation or gender identity in the previous 12 months, most often in the family, at school, and at work (iSEE, 2015). Human Rights Watch (2020) documented that school lessons frequently upheld the false belief that same-sex attraction is a mental disorder that can be diagnosed and cured. Pathologizing views, in other words, persist in the institutions that educate future workers. These reports, however, do not estimate associations between stigma and mental health.

Peer-reviewed quantitative studies serve a different function. Part of this literature has developed measurement tools, including a Vietnamese-language internalized homophobia scale for sexual minority women (T. Q. Nguyen, Poteat, et al., 2016) and an evaluation of the PHQ-9 as a measure of depressive symptom severity in the same population (T. Q. Nguyen, Bandeen-Roche, et al., 2016). Where these studies examine relationships, they concentrate on proximal processes. Tran et al. (2020) examined internalized homophobia, depressive symptoms, and self-disclosure, including disclosure to coworkers, among 302 sexual minority women. Ngo et al. (2026) found that self-stigma among 250 members of the LGBTQ+ community in northern and central Vietnam was associated with age, biological sex, and level of disclosure. Among transgender people, L. V. Nguyen et al. (2026) examined gender dysphoria, resilience, and psychological distress in a sample of 347. Among the studies reviewed, Ha et al. (2015) provide the clearest measurement of stigma from others: in a survey of 451 men who have sex with men in Hanoi, depression and substance use appeared as intermediate links between stigma and sexual risk behavior.

Two features characterize this peer-reviewed evidence. Distal stressors within a specific domain of life, such as the workplace, have not been measured systematically, and most studies examine a single sexual or gender minority group, typically sexual minority women, men who have sex with men, or transgender people. One recurring finding is that disclosure of identity emerges as an important variable in both Tran et al. (2020) and Ngo et al. (2026). This is consistent with the argument developed in Chapter 2 that concealment is a central process to account for when studying stigma in Vietnam.

### 1.4. The Research Problem

The preceding sections allow the research problem to be stated precisely. The mental health disparity between sexual minority and heterosexual people is well established, but it does not reveal its own cause. In Vietnam, pathologizing interpretations persist in public discourse and in some institutions, even though the health authority has formally rejected them. Stigma against LGBT people, including stigma at work, has been documented descriptively. What remains unknown is whether, among LGBT workers, more frequent stigma at work accompanies higher levels of anxiety and depressive symptoms.

The question matters on three grounds. Scientifically, it is a direct test of the central prediction of the prejudice account in a setting that differs from the one in which the prediction was developed. Practically, the workplace is an organized environment with identifiable responsible parties and available instruments of intervention, unlike the family or the wider community; evidence of an association would give employers and occupational health policymakers a basis for action. Interpretively, in a setting where LGBT people's symptoms can be read as evidence of pathology, evidence that symptoms track social conditions can correct that reading.

Answering the question requires a theory that explains why stigma at work might be associated with symptoms, through which pathways, and under what conditions. Chapter 2 develops that theory.

## CHAPTER 2. MINORITY STRESS AT WORK: THEORY, EVIDENCE, AND RESEARCH GAPS

Chapter 1 ended with a question: among LGBT workers, is stigma at work associated with higher symptom levels? This chapter develops the theoretical basis for answering it. It begins with minority stress theory and the predictions it generates (Section 2.1). It then explains why the workplace is a distinctive context for minority stress (Section 2.2), defines workplace stigma (Section 2.3), and examines the positions of identity concealment (Section 2.4) and perceived DEI enforcement (Section 2.5). Section 2.6 identifies the research gaps and introduces the present study.

### 2.1. Minority Stress Theory

#### 2.1.1. Structure and Extensions

Minority stress theory explains mental health disparities through social conditions rather than identity. I. H. Meyer (1995) examined three components of minority stress among gay men: internalized homophobia, perceived stigma, and prejudice events. I. H. Meyer (2003) later integrated these components into a general model for lesbian, gay, and bisexual populations. In this model, minority stress has three properties. It is unique, in the sense that it adds to the general stressors that everyone faces. It is chronic, because it is tied to relatively stable social and cultural structures. And it is socially based, because it originates in processes and institutions beyond the individual.

The model orders stressors by their distance from the person. Distal stressors are objective events and conditions, such as discrimination, harassment, and violence directed at a person because of their identity. Proximal stressors are subjective processes: expectations of rejection, concealment of identity, and internalized stigma. The distinction is theoretical rather than merely classificatory. Distal stressors exist in the environment, even though any survey measure of them passes through the respondent's perception. Proximal stressors represent the ways in which individuals internalize and cope with a stigmatizing environment, so they can persist in periods when no stigmatizing event occurs. The model also places coping resources and social support, at both the individual and the community level, in a position to alter the relationship between stress and health.

The original model left open the question of how stigma "gets under the skin." Hatzenbuehler's (2009) psychological mediation framework proposes that stigma-related stress heightens difficulties in emotion regulation, interpersonal functioning, and cognition, and that these general processes in turn lead to psychopathology. The framework connects minority stress to mechanisms already established in research on psychopathology more broadly. For empirical work, it carries an important implication: a distal stressor can be linked to symptoms through several pathways at once, of which the proximal stressors specific to minority status are only a part.

Two extensions of the theory are directly relevant to this study. The first concerns structural stigma, defined as societal conditions, cultural norms, and institutional policies that constrain the opportunities, resources, and well-being of stigmatized groups (Hatzenbuehler, 2016). Part of the evidence on structural stigma comes from longitudinal designs. Hatzenbuehler et al. (2010) found that psychiatric disorders increased among lesbian, gay, and bisexual people living in U.S. states that had recently passed constitutional amendments banning same-sex marriage. Other evidence comes from cross-national comparison: across 28 European countries, structural stigma was associated with greater concealment of sexual orientation and with lower life satisfaction among sexual minority people (Pachankis & Bränström, 2018). The second extension adapts the model to transgender and gender nonconforming people (Hendricks & Testa, 2012). Reviewing two decades of work, Frost and Meyer (2023) emphasize that minority stress processes must be studied across different and changing social and policy contexts. This emphasis is one reason to test the theory's predictions outside the settings in which it was developed.

#### 2.1.2. Testable Predictions and the State of the Evidence

Minority stress theory is a causal theory about processes that unfold over time. It yields at least three kinds of empirical prediction. The first concerns disparities: minority groups should show higher symptom levels than majority groups. The second concerns a within-group gradient: within a minority group, people exposed to more distal stressors should show higher symptom levels. The third concerns pathways: proximal stressors should lie between distal stressors and symptoms.

The three predictions place different demands on data. A test of disparities requires a comparison group recruited in an equivalent way. A test of pathways requires temporally ordered data, because cross-sectional mediation analyses can yield substantially biased estimates of processes that unfold over time (Maxwell & Cole, 2007). A test of the within-group gradient requires only variation in exposure among members of the group, which makes it the prediction that a cross-sectional survey of LGBT people can address most directly. It is also the only one of the three that sets the two readings described in Chapter 1 against each other. Both readings predict a disparity. The pathway prediction presupposes that distal stressors matter and is therefore not the first test. Only the gradient prediction separates an account that expects symptoms to vary with stigma among members of the same group from an account that does not.

Evidence bearing on the gradient prediction is stronger for concurrent than for prospective associations. Across stigmatized groups, studies that measure discrimination and symptoms at the same time consistently find that people who report more discrimination also report more symptoms (Lewis et al., 2015; Schmitt et al., 2014). Longitudinal evidence is more mixed. In a meta-analysis by Schmitt et al. (2014), perceived discrimination remained associated with lower well-being in longitudinal studies that controlled for earlier well-being (*r* = −.15). Among sexual and gender minority individuals assigned female at birth, minority stress at one wave predicted increases in anxiety and depressive symptoms six months later (Dyar et al., 2020, as cited in Herry & Dyar, 2025). In an eight-year study of young gay and bisexual men, by contrast, distal stressors were associated with symptoms measured at the same time but did not predict later change (Pachankis et al., 2018, as cited in Herry & Dyar, 2025). This pattern has a direct implication for the present study. A cross-sectional gradient establishes co-occurrence. It cannot show that stigma precedes symptoms, and the mixed prospective evidence is a reason to interpret any gradient with caution.

#### 2.1.3. Anxiety and Depression as Distinct Outcomes

Anxiety and depression are closely related but distinct. Factor analysis in the original development of the PHQ-4 identified separate anxiety and depression factors (Kroenke et al., 2009), and the same structure was observed in a German general population sample (Löwe et al., 2010). Theory suggests why stigma might relate to the two dimensions differently. Vigilance and the expectation of rejection are oriented toward future threats and are therefore plausibly linked to anxiety (I. H. Meyer, 2003; Pachankis, 2007). In a population-based longitudinal study of 811 Swedish sexual minority young adults, hypervigilance linked experiences of discrimination and victimization to internalizing symptoms two years later (Hollinsaid et al., 2023). A further possibility is that repeated stigma that a person cannot control is expressed in loss of interest and hopelessness, which are core features of depression. Empirically, Herry and Dyar (2025) found that baseline sexual orientation discrimination was associated with higher anxiety at follow-up after adjustment for baseline anxiety, whereas the corresponding association with depressive symptoms was smaller and less precisely estimated.

This reasoning justifies examining anxiety and depressive symptoms separately alongside total symptom severity. It does not justify a directional hypothesis that stigma is more strongly associated with one dimension than with the other. In the present study, each dimension is measured with only two items, and a difference in statistical significance between two coefficients is not evidence that the coefficients differ (Gelman & Stern, 2006). The study therefore treats the two dimensions in parallel.

### 2.2. The Workplace as a Minority Stress Context

#### 2.2.1. Structural Features of Work

Many studies of minority stress measure stigma across life in general rather than within a particular domain (e.g., Herry & Dyar, 2025). The workplace differs from other domains in four structural respects, which explain why distal stressors at work may operate differently from those encountered in public spaces.

Interaction at work is repeated and difficult to avoid. Workers meet the same colleagues and supervisors daily and over long periods. Leaving a stigmatizing environment is costly, because it means losing income or searching for another job. A stigmatizing act at work is therefore unlikely to be an isolated event, and workers have limited ability to remove themselves from exposure.

Workplaces are hierarchical systems for allocating resources. Supervisors decide on task assignments, performance evaluations, training opportunities, and pay. When the prejudice of a decision-maker is translated into a personnel decision, stigma ceases to be only an unpleasant interaction and becomes a formal disadvantage. Hebl et al. (2002) distinguished formal discrimination, such as hiring decisions, from interpersonal discrimination, such as coldness or shorter interactions. In their field experiment, job applicants presented as gay were not treated differently in formal terms but received more negative interpersonal responses. The two forms of stigma can thus occur independently, and a measure that records only formal decisions would miss an important part of workers' experience.

Work involves continuous evaluation by others who may hold prejudices. When an unfavorable decision occurs, a minority worker must judge whether it reflects their performance or prejudice against them. This attributional ambiguity is itself a source of stress. It is also a source of measurement error, because the same decision can be attributed to different causes depending on the respondent's expectations and mood.

Workplaces have rules and climates. Organizations can sanction, tolerate, or encourage stigmatizing behavior. Ragins and Cornwell (2001) found that gay and lesbian employees reported more discrimination when they worked in predominantly heterosexual work groups and in organizations that lacked supportive policies. The role of the organization is examined further in Section 2.5.

These four features have a theoretical consequence. The workplace is a setting in which interpersonal prejudice can be institutionalized as allocative decisions, so it allows distal stressors to be observed at two levels within the same context: interaction and decision. For minority stress theory, this specifies how a distal stressor can operate within an institutional environment. It does not imply that stigma at work is more strongly associated with symptoms than stigma in the family or in public. The present study has no data with which to compare domains of life.

#### 2.2.2. Evidence from Workplace Studies

Evidence on minority stress at work is consistent in direction but limited in design and context. In direction, experiences of heterosexism and discrimination at work accompany greater psychological distress (Velez et al., 2013; Waldo, 1999) and less favorable work attitudes (Ragins & Cornwell, 2001). A systematic review of 32 studies including 8,369 LGBTQ+ workers identified internalized stigma, heterosexism, job stress, and low income as factors associated with adverse mental health outcomes (Tomic et al., 2025). Some studies also consider job characteristics such as employment precarity, industry, and perceived workplace support for LGBTQ people (Owens et al., 2022), a point that bears on how the present study treats job characteristics in its models (Chapter 3).

In design, 30 of the 32 studies in the review by Tomic et al. (2025) were cross-sectional, and the review's main findings were expressed as differences between LGBTQ+ and non-LGBTQ+ workers. Such differences are consistent with minority stress theory, but they do not test the within-group prediction.

In context, the foundational studies were conducted in the United States, where legal protection varied across jurisdictions (Ragins & Cornwell, 2001; Velez et al., 2013; Waldo, 1999). Evidence from Asia has only recently begun to appear. In a survey of 706 LGBTQ employees in China, Lo et al. (2025) found that four minority stressors (experiences of discrimination, internalized homophobia, expectations of rejection, and concealment) were each associated with less favorable perceptions of workplace climate, which in turn were associated with more depressive symptoms. The study shows that workplace minority stress processes can be observed outside North America, although it measured depressive symptoms only.

### 2.3. Workplace Stigma

#### 2.3.1. Stigma, Discrimination, and Forms of Enacted Stigma

Stigma and discrimination are often used interchangeably, but their scope differs. Link and Phelan (2001) define stigma as the co-occurrence of labeling, stereotyping, separation, status loss, and discrimination in a situation of power that allows these components to unfold. On this definition, discrimination is one component of stigma, namely the behavioral component that produces disadvantage. Stigma is broader, because it also includes labeling and status-lowering behavior that need not be tied to an allocative decision.

This study uses the concept of enacted stigma: the manifestations of stigma that workers directly experience. Enacted stigma at work is defined here as behavior and conditions in the workplace that target, or are based on, a worker's sexual orientation, gender identity, or gender expression and that demean, intrude upon, or disadvantage the worker. The concept covers five forms:

- *Interpersonal derogation*, such as offensive jokes, comments, or name-calling. Part of this form overlaps with microaggressions: subtle and often seemingly harmless behaviors that communicate hostile or demeaning messages (Nadal et al., 2016).
- *Intrusion into privacy*, including unwanted personal questions and outing or threats of outing without consent. This form is specific to concealable identities, because it directly violates control over information about oneself.
- *Formal discrimination*, including exclusion from training opportunities, unfair evaluation or pay, and unfavorable work assignments.
- *Pressure to conform to gender stereotypes* in dress, speech, or behavior.
- *Barriers to gender identity*, such as difficulty using a name, pronouns, dress, or restrooms consistent with one's gender identity.

This typology answers a question that reviewers often raise: is workplace stigma really distinct from discrimination? Only the third form corresponds to discrimination in the narrow sense of behavior that impairs equality of opportunity in employment or occupation, which is close to the definition in Vietnam's Labor Code. The other forms are enacted stigma but not discrimination in the legal sense. The manuscript therefore uses "workplace stigma" for the overall construct and reserves "discrimination" for formal decisions or for studies that use that term.

#### 2.3.2. Pervasiveness and Attribution

Two properties of enacted stigma are theoretically important for this study. The first is pervasiveness. In Schmitt et al.'s (2014) meta-analysis, experimental studies that manipulated perceptions of how pervasive discrimination is produced lower well-being, whereas attributing a single negative event to discrimination rather than to oneself made no difference. The authors concluded that pervasiveness is fundamental to the harm of perceived discrimination. A measure of exposure consistent with this conclusion should record how often stigma occurs over a period of time, not only whether it has ever occurred.

The second property is dependence on attribution. Forms of stigma differ in how far the person who experiences them must infer the motives of others. An offensive joke about sexual orientation carries its stigmatizing content within itself. An unfavorable evaluation or pay decision, by contrast, counts as stigma only if the person believes that it was related to their sexual orientation or gender identity. Lewis et al. (2015) reviewed advances and unresolved debates in research on self-reported discrimination and health, including debates about measurement. For this study, the difference in attributional dependence has two consequences. Theoretically, formal discrimination may be linked to symptoms through ambiguity and perceived injustice, whereas interpersonal derogation may be linked to symptoms through direct messages of devaluation. For measurement, attribution-dependent forms are more susceptible to the influence of expectations and mood. If an association between stigma and symptoms appeared only for attribution-dependent forms, attribution or recall bias would need to be considered seriously.

These two properties guide how stigma is measured in this study: as a set of concrete situations, each recorded by frequency over 12 months, so that analyses can be conducted both for the overall index and for individual situations. Chapter 3 describes how the index was constructed, its limitations, and why it is not evaluated as a reflective scale.

### 2.4. Identity Concealment: A Proximal Stressor with Two Positions

Sexual orientation and, in many cases, gender identity are concealable. For people with concealable identities, the decision to disclose or conceal is made repeatedly, in interaction after interaction. Clair et al. (2005) describe the management of invisible identities at work as a series of choices between concealment and disclosure, shaped by individual characteristics and organizational context. Ragins et al. (2007) showed that fear of the consequences of disclosure is related to the work environment and to the support that workers perceive.

I. H. Meyer (2003) classifies concealment as a proximal stressor because it carries psychological costs. Pachankis's (2007) cognitive-affective-behavioral model describes those costs: concealment demands constant vigilance, preoccupation with the possibility of discovery, and self-monitoring, processes that can lead to anxiety, depression, and social isolation. The model also suggests a distinction between a behavioral component of concealment, which covers what a person does to keep information private, and an affective component, which covers the worry and fatigue that accompany it.

The evidence is more complex than the theory. A meta-analysis by Pachankis et al. (2020) found that the association between concealment of sexual orientation and depressive symptoms depended on the facet of concealment measured and on sample characteristics, with stronger associations in samples consisting only of bisexual people. Concealment is thus not uniformly harmful. It is also a coping strategy that workers may adopt deliberately to avoid stigma.

The most important point for this study is that concealment stands in a two-way position relative to enacted stigma. On one side, enacted stigma may lead workers to conceal more, and concealment, with its cognitive and emotional costs, may be accompanied by more symptoms. This is the pathway described by minority stress theory and examined in Hypothesis 3 (Section 3.1). On the other side, concealment may reduce the likelihood that others know about a worker's identity and, therefore, reduce exposure to forms of stigma directed at people whose identity is known. The two directions predict opposite signs for the association between stigma and concealment, and cross-sectional data cannot separate them.

The two-way position of concealment also affects how the main gradient should be read. If concealment both reduces exposure and carries its own psychological costs, workers who report little stigma may include people who conceal extensively and whose symptoms are not low. In that case, the observed gradient between stigma and symptoms could be smaller than the gradient that an effect of stigma, if one exists, would produce. This possibility deserves particular attention in settings where concealment may be common, such as those with higher structural stigma (Pachankis & Bränström, 2018). For these reasons, the study treats concealment as a hypothesized intermediate variable. It assesses only whether the data are consistent with the pathway from stigma to concealment to symptoms and does not interpret the results as evidence of mediation.

### 2.5. Perceived DEI Enforcement as an Organizational Condition

I. H. Meyer's (2003) model places coping resources and social support in a moderating position. The logic resembles the stress-buffering hypothesis of Cohen and Wills (1985), according to which social support weakens the association between stressors and health outcomes. In the workplace, the organization is a potential source of support, and there are at least three reasons to expect the association between stigma and symptoms to be weaker where workers perceive DEI policies to be enforced.

The first concerns the meaning of the event. When an organization reprimands or sanctions offensive behavior, a stigmatizing joke may be experienced as the act of an individual rather than as a signal that the organization accepts stigma. This argument connects with Schmitt et al.'s (2014) finding on pervasiveness: an event that the organization addresses is less likely to be understood as evidence of pervasive rejection. The second concerns recourse. A safe reporting channel gives workers an option other than enduring or leaving. The third concerns decision-makers. A direct manager who respects LGBT people may reduce the chance that prejudice is translated into personnel decisions.

The study measures perceived enforcement rather than the existence of written policies, a choice supported by both theory and evidence. Institutional theory describes decoupling: organizations may adopt formal structures to gain legitimacy without incorporating those structures into practice (J. W. Meyer & Rowan, 1977). Where labor law does not require policies against discrimination based on sexual orientation and gender identity, as in Vietnam (Section 1.3.1), the gap between written policy and practice may be wide. Empirically, a meta-analysis by Webster et al. (2018) compared three forms of workplace support: formal LGBT policies and practices, LGBT-supportive climate, and supportive workplace relationships. Supportive relationships were most strongly related to work attitudes and psychological strain, and supportive climate was most strongly related to disclosure and perceived discrimination; formal policies were not the strongest correlate for any of the four outcome groups. Huffman et al. (2008) similarly found that support from supervisors, coworkers, and the organization was related to different outcomes, so these sources need to be distinguished. The four aspects of enforcement measured in this study correspond to these components: safety in reporting discrimination, the attitude of the direct manager, the handling of offensive behavior, and the ability to use a name, pronouns, and dress consistent with one's gender identity.

Three limitations confine the analysis of DEI to exploratory status. Perceived enforcement is reported by workers themselves, so it reflects perception rather than an objective organizational attribute. The concepts of perceived enforcement and enacted stigma also overlap in content: whether offensive behavior is sanctioned is the organization's response to the very kind of event that the stigma index records, and the experience of stigma may itself lower workers' assessments of enforcement. Finally, tests of interaction in observational data have much lower statistical power than tests of main effects (McClelland & Judd, 1993). The study therefore treats perceived DEI enforcement as a condition that may accompany differences in a statistical association, not as a causal moderating mechanism.

### 2.6. Research Gaps and the Present Study

Synthesizing Chapter 1 and the preceding sections reveals three gaps. Each corresponds to a specific limitation of the existing evidence rather than simply to the absence of studies from Vietnam.

The first gap concerns the prediction being tested. Evidence on LGBT workers' mental health is reported mainly as differences between LGBT and non-LGBT workers (Tomic et al., 2025). As argued in Section 2.1.2, such differences cannot discriminate between the two readings, because both predict a disparity. The prediction that does discriminate between them is a within-group gradient in symptoms by exposure to stigma at work, and it is less often tested directly. In convenience samples, comparisons between groups are also vulnerable to bias from differences in recruitment channels.

The second gap concerns institutional context. The foundational studies of minority stress at work come from the United States, where variation in legal protection across states and in organizational policy has itself been an object of study (Ragins & Cornwell, 2001). Evidence from settings in which labor law does not name stigma based on sexual orientation and gender identity, in which concealment may be common, and in which pathologizing views remain present is limited. These features do more than make the setting new. They may change the conditions under which the prediction must hold: exposure depends more on the individual workplace, and concealment may narrow the observed gradient. To the author's knowledge, no peer-reviewed study in Vietnam has estimated the association between enacted stigma at work and anxiety or depressive symptoms among workers from several sexual and gender minority groups. This claim will be retained in any published version only after a structured literature search of Scopus, Web of Science, PubMed, Google Scholar, and Vietnamese journals has been completed and reported.

The third gap concerns measurement and inference. When stigma at work is summed into a single score, or combined with stigma in other life domains (e.g., Herry & Dyar, 2025), it becomes impossible to tell in which forms of behavior an association with symptoms is concentrated. The author has found no study of LGBT workers that uses the contrast between attribution-dependent and attribution-independent forms of stigma as a check on reporting bias. Some recent studies measure only depressive symptoms (Lo et al., 2025; Tran et al., 2020), although theory and prospective evidence suggest that anxiety and depression should be examined separately (Section 2.1.3). Finally, because most of the evidence in this field is cross-sectional, the added value of a new cross-sectional study depends on how transparent it is about its estimand, its analytic choices, and the sensitivity of its results to bias.

Table 2.1 maps each gap onto the study's approach and onto the corresponding analyses, which are stated formally in Chapter 3.

The present study addresses these gaps by testing whether, among LGBT workers in Vietnam, more frequent enacted stigma at work accompanies higher symptom levels. The primary hypothesis (H1) states the within-group gradient for total symptom severity.
